There is no cut-off

The ranges you see everywhere — 1400+, 1500+ — are the middle 50% of students who were admitted. They describe who got in, not who is allowed to apply. Students are admitted below those ranges every year, and rejected far above them.

So stop asking "is my score enough". Ask instead: who will pay for me.

The 10 need-blind universities

These US universities decide on your admission without looking at your ability to pay, and then commit to covering 100% of demonstrated financial need for international students. That combination is the most generous aid in the world.

  • Amherst
  • Bowdoin
  • Notre Dame
  • Washington and Lee
Everywhere else is need-aware for international students. That means asking for aid can affect the admission decision. Stanford, Penn and Cornell are generous — but they appear on "need-blind" lists by mistake all the time, and for international applicants they are not. Confirm the policy on each university's own financial aid page before you rely on it.

What to check on each university page

  1. Is the policy need-blind or need-aware for internationals? Universities word this carefully — look for "international" specifically, not the general statement.
  2. Does it meet 100% of demonstrated need? Need-blind without full-need aid means you can be admitted and still unable to pay.
  3. Which financial form is required — CSS Profile, ISFAA or their own — and its deadline, which is often earlier than the application deadline.
  4. Is the SAT required, optional or not considered? Many US universities stayed test-optional, and some no longer look at scores at all.

If your score is lower than you hoped

Merit scholarships at large public universities are driven more by GPA than by SAT, and several countries admit without any standardised test at all. A lower score narrows your list, it does not end it.
